How Pennant Math works

What this site does

Pennant Math looks past the standings. It asks how well each team is really playing, how much of its record is luck, and how the rest of the season could go. Measures marked Pennant Math original are our own constructions, not official statistics.

Where the data comes from

Game dates and final scores come from BALLDONTLIE. Everything else is computed here from those scores. Game times are converted to Eastern time, and the All-Star Game is left out. Two games were never made up and are accounted for: the 2024 Astros at Guardians finale and the 2026 Orioles at Yankees finale. During the season the site refreshes itself every morning. Every update checks that there are 30 teams, no duplicate games, no tied finals, and no impossible totals, and compares four verified final records against the computed ones. If a check fails, nothing is published.

Run differential and luck

Run differential is runs scored minus runs allowed. It predicts future results better than the win-loss record does. Expected wins use the Pythagenpat formula: expected win% = RSx / (RSx + RAx) with x = ((RS + RA) / games)0.287. Luck is actual wins minus expected wins. A positive number means a team won more than its runs justify, and those teams tend to slip back. It is an estimate, not a verdict.

Elo ratings Pennant Math original

Every team starts at 1500, the average, at the start of 2023. After each game the winner gains and the loser loses 4 × ln(margin + 1) × (result − expected), so beating a stronger team or winning big moves a rating more. The home team gets a 24-point edge in the prediction. Each offseason, teams keep two-thirds of their distance from 1500. Postseason games count. These settings are reasonable starting values, not heavily tuned, and 2023 is a warm-up year because the ratings start with no history.

Playoff odds Pennant Math original

As of any date, games already played are fixed and every remaining game is simulated 10,000 times, with each game's chance set by the two teams' Elo ratings. Each league sends six teams: three division winners and three wild cards. A team's odds are the share of simulations in which it gets in. Because ratings are estimates, every simulation gives each team a slightly different true strength (about 30 rating points of uncertainty), which keeps favorites and long shots from looking too certain. Ties in wins are broken by head-to-head record, then division record, then a random draw, a simplification of MLB's full rules. Once a season is over, the page shows the final playoff field instead.

Pennant Score Pennant Math original

A single 0 to 100 grade. Each team is ranked among the 30 on three things, and the ranks are blended: Results (30%, run differential per game), Underlying strength (45%, Elo rating and expected win%), and Outlook (25%, playoff odds and how easy the remaining schedule is). Outlook exists only while a season is in progress; when it is missing, the other two are reweighted. 100 is the best team and 0 the weakest. The weights are our judgment, not a proven formula, and will be checked against how well the score predicts the rest of a season.

Other team measures

One-run record is wins and losses in games decided by one run. Records far from .500 tend to drift back, so a team more than about 1.5 standard deviations from an even record gets an arrow: ↓ lucky and likely to slip, ↑ unlucky and likely to improve. That is a rule of thumb, and one-run results are only weakly repeatable. Home and road run differential compare scoring at home and away; they hint at park effects but are small samples. Record against teams above .500 uses the standings at the time to decide who counts as a winning team. Schedule played is the average rating of the opponents faced so far, relative to the league average, using each team's latest rating; positive means a harder schedule.

The category pages

Offense, Pitching & defense, Game flow, and Close games are built from team totals only: runs, hits, errors, shutouts, and margins of victory. The free data has no batting average, walks, strikeouts, fielding range, or play-by-play, so these pages show what team totals can honestly say and note what they cannot. Close-game records are mostly luck, and true clutch performance needs data we do not have.

Game flow uses each game's inning-by-inning scores. A comeback win is a win after trailing through six innings; a blown lead is a loss after leading through six; a walk-off is a home win in the team's final turn at bat; and a heartbreak loss is either a blown lead or a walk-off loss. The scoring-by-inning columns on the Offense and Pitching & defense pages use the same line scores. Games whose inning scores do not add up to the final score are left out of these numbers.

How well do the odds work?

We replayed 2023 through 2026 week by week, hiding everything after each date, and scored the odds against who really made the playoffs.

  • The model's final playoff field matched the teams that actually played in the postseason in all four seasons.
  • Its Brier score (lower is better) was about 0.12, against about 0.19 for "is in a playoff spot right now" and 0.24 for giving every team 40 percent.
  • Predicted and actual playoff rates differ by about 3 percentage points on average. Before adding the rating uncertainty, favorites were overrated and long shots underrated.

Four seasons is a small sample, about 120 team-seasons, so this is encouraging evidence, not proof.

What it does not know

  • Injuries, trades, free-agent signings, and preseason projections. Ratings come only from past results.
  • The full MLB tiebreak rules.
  • Anything about the future of a player. Odds are estimates for entertainment and analysis, not betting advice.
